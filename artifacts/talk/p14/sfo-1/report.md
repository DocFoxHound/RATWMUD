## Voices (one line each: what makes this wolf unlike any other in the town)
- sm104 the quiet tally: keeps the taproom and lives over it: quiet, plain and even, forever counting cups, casks and stars under the breath, and saving penny by penny toward a few acres and a pear tree of one's own. Oath "Tap and tally"; saying "A slow tap fills a true jar".
- sm105 the long-road pot hand: scrubs pots and floors with a patience worn smooth: quiet, tired and plain, counting each pot under the breath, notching a whittled tally stick by the fire, and never boasting of the long walk north and home again. Oath "Suds and splinters"; saying "Every road has a back half".
- sm106 the doubting giver: shares the last of the bread without being asked and trusts nothing that came off a city cart without turning it over twice: warm, wary and plain, never far from a whittling knife, and set on grafting trees of one's own. Oath "Crumbs and kindling"; saying "Look at the underside first".
- sm107 the cellar proverb-maker: keeps the casks in the press-house cellar: dry, sly and plain, with an old saying for everything, half made up on the spot and sworn to be ancient, and a firm belief that the standing stones on the downs remember all of it. Oath "Stones remember"; saying "What's buried isn't gone".
- lf138 the late collier: burns charcoal up at the hearths, gives away the best of every supper and turns up everywhere a little after everyone else: quiet, warm and tired to the bone, with a sharp knife always on the belt and an eye for a wide sky. Oath "Oh, the hour"; saying "Late is still arrived".
- sm108 the river-led vine hand: tends the vines on the slope, calm and plain and honest to a fault, carries a smooth stone from a wood stream in one pocket, and leaves another on the empty chapel's sill whenever there's something to be thankful for. Oath "River lead me"; saying "Follow the water and you're never lost long".
- oh102 the favour-keeper: fells oaks at the cut and says little: devout, close as bark, wary of new faces, setting a riddle now and then, and keeping a notched stick of every favour given and owed, squared to the last notch. Oath "Root and reckoning"; saying "A favour's a seed; it comes up in its season".
- sm109 the slow-dance farmer: farms the home fields at an unhurried pace, says a thing straight and once, watches the swallows on the eaves, and has never sat out a harvest dance: the slow one at the end is always taken. Oath "Rind and rafter"; saying "Say it once and say it straight".
- cw162 the travelled stone: came up from the lake country and never quite stopped moving: shrewd and close, quick to weigh rock and wolves alike, forever turning a smooth stone in one paw while deciding something, and watching the birds on the roofs as if the birds knew the way out. Oath "Lake and lode"; saying "A stone goes where the water sends it".
- fh101 the riddler by the wall: burns charcoal up at the hearths and always takes the seat facing the door: quiet, warm and close-mouthed, slow to say a thing twice, with a riddle kept back for anyone who sits long enough. Oath "Back to the wall"; saying "A shut mouth gathers no dust".
- sm110 the proverb beekeeper: keeps bees along the hedges and the larder full to the rafters: dry, plain and restless, never short of an old saying for any occasion, happiest with a hand of cards by the fire and a roast on the spit. Oath "Comb and candle"; saying "Wax remembers every flower".
- sm111 the north-road doubter: works the fields out of the granary: quiet, tired and restless, slow to trust anything that came down the north road, singing low in the rows, and feeling the old ones of the family watching every cut. Oath "By the whetstone and the stook"; saying "Nothing from up north comes without a hook in it".
- oh103 the storm-watcher: drags logs at the cut and frets at the sky: warm and anxious, quick to ask how you are and slow to believe the answer, awake half the night before any storm, and happiest of an evening with a line in the brook pool and an old map spread on the knees. Oath "Mercy on the leaves"; saying "Better a roof checked twice than once".
- sm112 the crumb-pocket hive hand: works the hives for a keeper's wage, keeps an apron pocket full of crumbs for the sparrows on the shed roof, says little and says it dry, and is quietly proud of every stick that was earned from nothing. Oath "Crumbs and comb"; saying "A full sparrow leaves the bees be".
- sm113 the wagering grinder: grinds corn and keeps the door shut: cold, blunt and guarded, will lay a penny on anything from the rain to which rook leaves the roof first, and knows a thing about one of the great Houses that is never said aloud. Oath "Grit and groat"; saying "Lay your penny and hold your tongue".
- sm114 the dancing storekeeper: chalks every price on a slate by the door and won't sweeten a word of it: dry, frank and even, doing the sums for an orchard not yet planted, and first on the boards at every harvest dance. Oath "Chalk and cider"; saying "Chalk rubs out, and so does a grudge".
- fh102 the pebble-pocket: digs ore up at the mine and fills every pocket with smooth stones off the riverbank: quick to flare and quicker to say sorry, warm underneath, and forever looking down the pass road in winter. Oath "River take it"; saying "Water's patient, rock never is".
- sm115 the thrice-told farmhand: works the fields out of the granary, loud and in no hurry, with three good tales told so often the whole lane could finish every one, and an eye that drifts up the north road whenever winter comes. Oath "Furrow and fair"; saying "A good tale wants telling twice".
- sm116 the knotted-string reaper: reaps in the barley out of the granary and ties a knot in a length of string for every sheaf, day and coin: blunt, tired and frank, fond of a hard riddle and a white frost, and putting something by that nobody is told about. Oath "By the ninth knot"; saying "A knot holds what a word lets slip".
- sm117 the potter who walked back: throws pots behind the kilns, slow and careful, with a thumb pressed into the foot of every one: quiet, warm and plain, wary of anything made in the city since that long walk north and back, and never without a plum tart to share. Oath "Wheel and kiln"; saying "A thumbprint is a promise in clay".
- sm118 the carter who comes late: drives grain up the north road behind two patient oxen and gets everywhere a little after the hour, sorry every time: warm, steady and plain, glad of a bowl of barley soup, a turn at a dance and a stiff new pair of boots. Oath "By the near ox"; saying "Better late in than spilt on the road".
- fh103 the downhill carter: drives the carts up and down the pass road and sits with a wall behind and the door in view, the way a carter watches the bend: quiet, dry as a biscuit, warm under it, and hard to beat at draughts. Oath "Brake and bend"; saying "Downhill's where it gets you".
- fh104 the lime-reckoner: burns lime up at the high quarry, sums up every load in one's head and means to own a kiln one day: calm, plain and unhurried, slow to warm, and quietly certain that nothing made in the city lasts up here. Oath "Kiln and quarry"; saying "Slow fire, sound lime".
- fh105 the hearth-counter: burns charcoal up at the hearths and lives above the hearths in a house kept just so, counting sacks, stairs and everything else under the breath: talkative about the work, short with mess and noise, and slow to say anything about the self. Oath "Soot and sums"; saying "Count it twice, burn it once".
- fh106 the boot-reader: keeps the store under the pass road and sleeps over the stock: patient, shrewd and close, reading every customer by the boots before a word is said, ink on the forepaws from the ledger, and a purse put by for a day nobody is told of. Oath "Ink and ice"; saying "Boots tell what the wolf won't".
- fh107 the bird-carver: keeps the taproom under the pit road and sleeps over it: gentle, open-pawed and unhurried, with a bowl and a seat by the fire for anyone off the shift, and a little wooden bird whittled for anyone who looks in need of one. Oath "Feather and flint"; saying "There's room for one more by any fire".
- oh104 the sky's quarreller: logs at the cut and has a running quarrel with the sky: quiet, dry and in no hurry, fond of a market at full noise and a tale told slow, and liable to tell a raincloud to its face exactly what it is. Oath "Sky and stump"; saying "The weather's no manners, but it always turns up".
- oh105 the carter who knows your gran: hauls a cart up and down the south road and has a firm opinion about everybody's grandmother: quick to bristle, proud of a sharp knife and a straight load, warm under the scowl, and close as a shut box about money owed. Oath "Shafts and axles"; saying "Every wolf's their gran over again".
- oh106 the logger who begs the tree's pardon: fells timber at the cut with an axe that has three names burned in the haft, and asks every tree's pardon before the first stroke: warm, quick to feed a stranger, shrewd as a squirrel about a full larder, and fond of an old tale told by the fire. Oath "Haft and heartwood"; saying "Ask the tree, then take it".
- oh107 the taproom tally-keeper: keeps a taproom and counts everything in it under the breath, cups and coppers and the gap before the thunder: blunt, jumpy, honest at the taps, and saving every spare copper for the city. Oath "Hoops and staves"; saying "Count it twice and pour it once".
- oh108 the shelf-squaring storekeeper: keeps a store with every jar squared to the shelf's edge: careful, shrewd and close with a fact, sharp at a bargain, suspicious of anything that came up the road from the city, and forever carving small birds to give away. Oath "Level and square"; saying "Turn it over before you trust it".
- oh109 the backward-reading printer: keeps a country press and counts every sheet under the breath: cold to a face not known, sparing with words and exact with letters, honest to a fault, and never without a block of wood and a gouge by the fire. Oath "Ink and oak"; saying "Ink can't take a word back".
- sm119 the clay-digger by the door: works hard and laughs at it, talks the ears off anyone at the table, always takes the seat facing the door, and knows one story about a big House that will never be told. Oath "By my muddy elbows"; saying "Sit facing the door and you'll never miss a friend".
- sm120 the vine-keeper with the stone crock: speaks plainly, bristles at haggling and early bells, whittles of an evening, and drops one smooth river stone into a crock for every coin put by, toward something nobody has been told. Oath "Stems and stones"; saying "Plain words keep longer than sweet ones".
- sm121 the hand who keeps step: does every job square and once, hums the old marching songs under the breath, carves pegs of an evening, and is proud of it all without saying so. Oath "Left, right, and home"; saying "Keep step and you'll keep up".
- sm122 the pup who keeps names: is quiet and dreamy, knows the name of every hen, horse and neighbour ever met, keeps apple pips in a tin, and means to plant the biggest orchard in the south. Oath "Pips and promises"; saying "Every orchard was one pip once".
- oh110 the bird-carving potter: carves little wooden birds of an evening to give away, talks a long tale and trims the truth to fit it, and knocks on anything off a city cart before trusting it an inch. Oath "Knot and kiln"; saying "Trust what you can knock on".
