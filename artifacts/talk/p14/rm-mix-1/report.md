- sw114 the sand-scoured tanner: works the vats at a city tannery and climbs to a room under the roof: guarded, quiet and watchful, the Sump's wariness laid over the Waste's manners, a pouch of dry sand to scour the stink off before going home, fond of honey cakes, honest dice and a chapel with nobody in it. Oath "By the dry pouch"; saying "Sand scours what water only spreads".
- fh108 the first-fruit stallholder: sells fruit off a stall on the square and lays the first of every crate aside for the chapel: devout, dry and close-mouthed, at war with wet boards and rats, judging everything by how long it will keep, and fond of an old tale told slowly. Oath "Bless the bough and the basket"; saying "Everything ripe is in a hurry".
- mb121 the gutter-boat carpenter: keeps a carpenter's bench in a city market hall, a long way from the fens of home: quiet, devout and dreamy, plain in every dealing, fond of plum tart and a slow game of draughts, and forever setting a little offcut boat in the gutter with a prayer in it. Oath "Peg and punt"; saying "A dry joint outlasts the paw that cut it".
- fh109 the slip-pinner: keeps a provision shop in the Commons and pins every slip the City Office hands over to the beam above the counter: dry, plain and driven, stocking up as if a mountain pass might close tomorrow, never haggling, and always one riddle ahead of the customer. Oath "Stamped and lost"; saying "The price is on the shelf, not in your mouth".
- sw115 the Restday gate-gazer: mixes plaster at a works yard and lives low in the city: patient and anxious, reserved and courteous in the slow Saltwell way, a worrier who checks everything twice, walking up the hill every Restday to look through the gates of the Heights, meaning to die up there, fond of blackberries, a swim and thunder heard from under a sound roof. Oath "By the white gate"; saying "A step a year is still a climb".
- sw116 the host of the lime yard: keeps a works yard in the rain city and answers for every load that leaves it: warm, patient and plain-spoken, the old Saltwell courtesy kept in a wet town, a cup of water poured for every caller before a word of business, fond of warm bread, a long summer evening and a feast-night dance. Oath "By lime and well-water"; saying "Give the guest water and the wall time".
- fh110 the smoke-loft provisioner: keeps the stores counted to the last sack and smells of the smoke loft whatever the weather: short with time-wasters, close about money, quick to know who is behind on paying and slow to say so, and still homesick for the quiet of a charcoal camp up in the hills. Oath "Cinders and salt"; saying "Every sack has a name on it".
- mb122 the step-sitter who blames the smoke: sits on a step in the Sump and watches the city go by, fen-born and a long way from the reeds: dry, prickly and plain, quick to take a slight, keeping one swept room and a crock of pickled herring, and blaming every cough on the city's chimneys and never the damp. Oath "Bog and soot"; saying "A step's as good as a window, and cheaper".
- mb123 the reed-whistler behind the counter: helps behind the counter of a Sump odds-and-ends shop, a long way from the fens of home: chatty, merry and honest to a fault, quick to tell you the plain truth and laugh about it after, fond of dice, fresh straw and a warm rain, and piping on a reed whistle whenever the shop goes quiet. Oath "Frogs and fours"; saying "Every throw's a fresh throw".
- fh111 the heel-giver: works the ovens in the Sump and slips the heels of loaves into hungry pockets without a word, then swears it never happened: quiet, warm and close, unhappy in the smoke, at ease on a long walk, and saying nothing about the self that need not be said. Oath "Crumbs and quiet"; saying "A full belly asks no questions".
- mb124 the warm-roll baker: bakes for a city bakehouse and lives over the ovens, a long way from the fens of home: quiet and warm, slow to speak and quick with bread, fond of a mist at dawn, a keen blade and a riddle of home-made sort, and never lets anybody stand dripping at the counter without a warm roll in the paw. Oath "Leaven and mist"; saying "A wet coat and a warm roll cancel out".
- mb125 the thunder-thanker: lives over the fish in the low streets, thanks the Bright One aloud whenever it thunders, and means to end somewhere higher and drier: gentle, devout and plain, wary of the Watch and generous with what little there is. Oath "Bright One and the high ground"; saying "Higher is drier, in this world and the next".
- oh111 the notice-reader: sets type at a press in the Commons and lives over it: devout, exact and plain-spoken, with no time for rudeness, who stops at every notice on every wall to read it for misprints, and saves the evenings for prayer, hard cheese, the stars and a storm heard from indoors. Oath "Line and letter"; saying "A misprint is a small lie".
- mb126 the window-book keeper: sits by the window with a pencil and a little book and writes down the street as it goes by: unhurried, curious and talkative about what is seen, careful about what is said, and fond of everything in its proper place. Oath "Page and pane"; saying "Watch the small things; the big ones announce themselves".
- sw117 the pulse-counter: keeps a sick ward for the abbey above the Sump stairs: cold, guarded and honest to the bone, a door kept shut to faces with no name, every pulse counted to an old caravan rhyme from the Waste, fond of clean straw, a frosty morning and the rare clear night of stars. Oath "Stars and clean linen"; saying "The pulse never lies, only the mouth".
- mb127 the high-step watcher: climbs to the highest step in reach to watch the ships come up the water and means to end up living higher still: blunt, patient and steady, frank to a fault, fond of a hard cheese and a riddle that takes a week. Oath "Rind and reason"; saying "Every riddle has a floor to it".
- sw118 the puddle-namer at the forge: works hot iron all day and names every puddle in the street after a well from home: chatty, warm and full of jokes, generous with apples, and saving toward a berth on a ship bound somewhere dry. Oath "Sparks and sand"; saying "Every puddle's a well to somebody".
- mb128 the reed in every sack: keeps the stores counted to the last sack and ties a twist of dry reed into the neck of each for luck: warm, plain and loyal, quick to feed whoever is at the counter and slow to say who owes what. Oath "Reed and rye"; saying "A tied sack spills nothing".
- mb129 the water-taster at the still: runs the copper still and tastes every water before trusting it: warm under a dry tongue, plain and patient, fond of a song and a bramble, with a tin of coppers saved for a market stall of the wearer's own. Oath "Copper and cork"; saying "Good spirit starts as good water".
- oh112 the chandler who rolls for it: minds the counter of a chandlery on the Wharf: quick-talking, merry and ambitious, private under all the chatter, with a price tag on every coil so nobody need haggle, a small die in the waistcoat pocket for every choice too small to think about, and a soft spot for honey cakes, thunder heard from indoors and a chapel with nobody in it. Oath "By the top face"; saying "The die never haggles".
- sw119 the loaf-sharer at the stills: works among the stills and breaks every loaf in two for whoever stands nearest: calm, slow-spoken and stubborn as a well-stone, content with little, fond of a good wrestling fall, and quietly homesick for the hush of the sand. Oath "By the warm loaf"; saying "Bread broken is bread doubled".
- fh112 the weight-guesser: carries loads across the square for a copper or two and calls out the weight of every one before lifting it, keeping a running tally of the guesses: chatty, merry and exact, fond of cards and a sharp knife, and dreaming of a ship to somewhere dry. Oath "Strap and stair"; saying "Nothing's heavy till you've guessed it".
- sw120 the salt-taster at the benches: guts and salts at the long benches and tastes every sack of salt before it goes near a fish: watchful, close-mouthed and shrewd, quick to see an angle and slow to say it, and never done wanting a salting shed of one's own. Oath "By the white pan"; saying "Taste it before you trust it".
- mb130 the stroke-counter: works the boats out of a city cove and counts everything under the breath, oar strokes, stairs, stars and coppers: blunt, tired and frank, quiet till asked, fen-born and broad, and grateful to anyone who ever put bread on the table. Oath "Nine and ninety"; saying "The tally's the tally, sore back or no".
- fh113 the sleeve-airer: helps behind the shelves, keeps every jar and cloth in its proper place, and hangs each coat on the sill to air the smoke out of it: restless, exact and plain-spoken, polite in the Estate way and broad underneath, with a fishing line kept ready for any quiet evening by the water. Oath "Clear the flue"; saying "A thing put back is a thing found".
- mb131 the round-the-block walker: walks the city end to end with no errand, and goes once round the block before answering any word said crooked: patient, plain and steady, quick to feel a slight and slow to say so, fen-born, with an apple in one pocket and a sharp knife in the other. Oath "Core and cobble"; saying "Walk it round before you say it".
- sw121 the churner who keeps time: churns butter at a dairy by the river and keeps the beat with an old marching tune hummed under the breath: quiet, blunt and a little far away, true to every word once given, fond of roast pork, an hour with a spade, a white frosty morning and the chapel before anyone else comes in. Oath "By the dasher"; saying "A vow is a well you keep drawing from".
- sw122 the deckhand who pays in verses: crews a boat out of a cove on the Sound: gentle, calm and honest to a fault, never in a hurry, who can't turn down a wager and pays every lost one with a song, and who loves honey cakes, old maps and a summer evening that won't end. Oath "By tide and tune"; saying "Lose a wager, pay a verse".
- fh114 the orchard hummer: works the orchard rows at a marching pace, humming the old songs under the breath to keep the step: warm, unhurried and open-pawed, quick to pour something hot and spiced for a visitor, fond of a throw of the dice, and true to a promise once it's made. Oath "Bough and bugle"; saying "A tune halves the row".
- oh113 the herd's pleader: drives the herd out and back in all weathers and bargains with the sky on the cows' behalf, half under the breath, then asks the Bright One's pardon for it: quiet, devout and bone-tired, slow to speak and slower to complain, with an old unanswered letter kept folded in a coat pocket. Oath "Bright One spare the byre"; saying "Ask the rain nicely and it rains anyway".
- sw123 the docker saving in a boot: hauls cargo off the ships at the piers and lives down in the Sump: blunt, jumpy and careful, never one to grumble where it can be overheard, saving every spare copper in the toe of a new boot that won't be worn until there's a market stall to wear it to, and fond of fancy knots, a honey cake on payday and thunder heard from bed. Oath "By the bowline"; saying "Coin by coin, the counter comes".
- sw124 the host who quotes you back: keeps a great house and its long table: gracious, exact and unhurried, with a memory for every word a guest has ever said there and the patience to repeat it at the right moment. Oath "By the long table"; saying "A guest leaves something at every table".
- mb132 the puddle-sounder: sounds every puddle with a stick before stepping in it and is in no hurry about anything else: fen-born, quiet, dry and even, slow to believe what floats up, fond of a board of draughts and fish out of a butter pan. Oath "Silt and sounding-pole"; saying "Sound it before you stand on it".
- fh115 the face-taker: walks the city end to end with a little pocket book, writing down every face that doesn't belong: blunt, cool and hard to fool, short with bad manners, plain to the point of rudeness, and saving every thought for a ship out of the Sound to somewhere it never rains. Oath "Bite the ice"; saying "Watch the paws, not the face".
- oh114 the snow-nose: fells timber out at the cut, walks home to a city room at dusk, and can smell snow coming a full day ahead: warm, frank and plain, honest past the point of sense, low some days and steady every day, quick to split a loaf with whoever is nearest.
